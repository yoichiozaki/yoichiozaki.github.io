## Introduction

Since the release of ChatGPT, large language models (LLMs) have come into wide use. They write code, summarize papers, and answer complex questions, and all of this comes from repeating one simple operation: predicting the next token.

This article traces the path from Word2Vec to GPT-4, then explains how text is split into tokens, the components of the Transformer and the self-attention mechanism at its center, how the next token is chosen (temperature, top-k, top-p), and how training proceeds from pre-training to RLHF.

## The LLM Genealogy — A Decade of Evolution

<LLMTimeline />

### The Word2Vec Era (2013)

[Mikolov et al. (2013)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781) proposed Word2Vec, which converts words into fixed-dimensional vectors (<strong>embeddings</strong>). In the famous example `king - man + woman ≈ queen`, relationships between word meanings show up as vector arithmetic. However, each word gets the same vector regardless of context (a static embedding).

### The Arrival of the Transformer (2017)

In "Attention Is All You Need," [Vaswani et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762) replaced the sequential processing of RNNs with <strong>self-attention</strong>, which lets all positions in a sequence be computed in parallel. This architecture is the foundation of today's LLMs.

### The Pre-training Era (2018–)

Three architecture families grew out of the Transformer:

<LLMFamilyTree />

#### Encoder-only (BERT family)

[BERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805) (2018) is pre-trained with <strong>Masked Language Modeling</strong> (MLM), predicting masked tokens. Because it captures bidirectional context, it does well on understanding tasks like classification, named-entity recognition, and similarity computation.

#### Encoder-Decoder (T5 family)

[T5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683) (2019) treats every task, including translation, summarization, and question answering, in the same text-to-text format: text in, text out.

#### Decoder-only (GPT family)

The GPT series uses autoregressive language models, generating one token at a time from left to right. Performance improved substantially as parameter counts grew, and GPT-3 (175B parameters) demonstrated few-shot learning: performing a new task from a few examples given in the prompt. Today's ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini all belong to this decoder-only, autoregressive family, though they are separate product and model lineages.

### Scaling Laws

<ScalingLawsDiagram />

[Kaplan et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361) discovered that language model loss follows a power law with respect to parameters $N$, data $D$, and compute $C$:

$$
L(x) \propto x^{-\alpha}
$$

Here $x$ is one of $N$, $D$, or $C$, and the law holds when the other two are not the bottleneck. The exponent $\alpha$ differs for each quantity.

[Hoffmann et al. (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556) (the Chinchilla paper) showed that compute-optimal dense language models need far more training tokens than many earlier large models used — roughly 20 training tokens per parameter in their setup. A 70B-parameter model, for example, corresponds to about 1.4 trillion training tokens.

## Tokenization — Converting Text to Numbers

LLMs split text into units called <strong>tokens</strong> and process it as a sequence of integer token IDs.

<BPETokenizationDiagram />

### BPE (Byte Pair Encoding)

The most widely used tokenization method in modern LLMs is BPE ([Sennrich et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.07909)). BPE builds its vocabulary as follows:

1. Split text into characters (or bytes)
2. Find the pair that appears next to each other most often in the corpus
3. Merge that pair into a new token
4. Repeat steps 2–3 until the vocabulary reaches the target size

```python
# Simplified BPE pseudocode
vocab = set(all_characters)
for i in range(num_merges):
    # Find the most frequent pair
    pair = most_frequent_pair(corpus)
    # Merge into new token
    new_token = pair[0] + pair[1]
    vocab.add(new_token)
    corpus = merge_in_corpus(corpus, pair, new_token)
```

GPT-4 has a vocabulary of approximately 100,000 tokens. With byte-level BPE, languages with many distinct characters, such as Japanese, often have less frequent characters split into more than one token each.

### Why Subwords?

A word-level vocabulary is large and cannot represent words unseen during training (the out-of-vocabulary, or OOV, problem). A character-level vocabulary is small and has no unknown words, but sequences become long. Subwords sit between the two: the vocabulary stays at a target size, and unseen words can still be represented as combinations of subwords. The same BPE tokenizer can also handle text in multiple languages.

## Transformer Architecture

Most LLMs use a decoder-only Transformer, shown below.

<TransformerArchitecture />

A decoder-only Transformer consists of input embeddings, a stack of N Transformer blocks (self-attention, FFN, residual connections, and normalization), and an output layer. The following sections divide this into five parts.

### 1. Input Embedding + Positional Encoding

The embedding layer converts token IDs into high-dimensional vectors (e.g., 4096 dimensions). An RNN reads its input one token at a time, so order is built into the computation. Self-attention processes all tokens in parallel and by itself does not distinguish their order, so positional information must be added explicitly via positional encoding.

#### Sinusoidal Positional Encoding (Original)

The Transformer of [Vaswani et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762) used sinusoidal positional encoding, where $pos$ is the position, $i$ is the dimension index, and $d$ is the embedding dimension:

$$
\text{PE}(pos, 2i) = \sin\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\right), \quad \text{PE}(pos, 2i+1) = \cos\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)
$$

In the heatmap below, lower dimensions (left) oscillate quickly with position (high frequency), while higher dimensions (right) change slowly (low frequency).

<PositionalEncodingDiagram />

With sinusoids, $\text{PE}(pos + k)$ can be expressed as a linear function of $\text{PE}(pos)$ for any fixed offset $k$. The original paper hypothesized that this property would make it easy for the model to learn relative positions.

#### RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding)

Many current LLMs, including LLaMA, Gemma, and Mistral, use [RoPE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09864). RoPE groups the dimensions of the query and key vectors into pairs and rotates each pair in its 2D plane by an angle that depends on the position.

<RoPEDiagram />

RoPE's key property is that <strong>the dot product of the rotated query $q_m$ and key $k_n$ depends on position only through the relative position $(m - n)$</strong>. Each token is rotated according to its absolute position, but the attention score reflects only the distance between the two tokens.

$$
\text{RoPE}(x, pos) = \begin{pmatrix} x_0 \cos(\theta \cdot pos) - x_1 \sin(\theta \cdot pos) \\ x_0 \sin(\theta \cdot pos) + x_1 \cos(\theta \cdot pos) \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix}
$$

Applied unchanged to sequences longer than those seen in training, RoPE tends to degrade, but adjusting its rotation frequencies lets it handle longer sequences. Context-extension methods such as NTK-aware scaling and YaRN are based on this adjustment.

### 2. Multi-Head Self-Attention

The attention computation itself is covered in the next section, "Self-Attention"; this section explains why a Transformer uses multiple heads.

A single attention head computes only one set of attention weights for each token. With multiple heads, different heads can learn different relationships in parallel, such as subject-verb agreement, adjective-noun modification, and coreference.

<MultiHeadAttentionDiagram />

$$
\text{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_h) \cdot W_O
$$

where $\text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(Q W_Q^{(i)}, K W_K^{(i)}, V W_V^{(i)})$. GPT-3 uses 96 heads; LLaMA 2 70B uses 64 heads.

#### MQA / GQA — Optimizing Inference Efficiency

In standard multi-head attention (MHA), each head has its own K and V projections. The KV cache stored during inference (described later) therefore grows with the number of heads. MQA and GQA shrink it by sharing K and V across several Q heads.

| Method | K,V Heads | Model Examples | Characteristics |
|--------|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| MHA | = Q heads | GPT-3 | Highest quality, largest KV cache |
| MQA | 1 | PaLM, StarCoder | All heads share K,V; fastest inference |
| GQA | Q / g | LLaMA 2 70B, Mistral | Between MHA and MQA |

GQA (Grouped-Query Attention) divides the Q heads into groups of $g$ and shares K,V within each group. LLaMA 2 70B adopted GQA and achieved near-MHA quality with significantly faster inference.

### 3. Residual Connections & Layer Normalization

In each sublayer (self-attention and FFN), the input is added to the output (a <strong>residual connection</strong>) and combined with normalization. Where the normalization goes differs between Post-LN and Pre-LN, described below.

#### Why Residual Connections Are Essential

Without residual connections, backpropagated gradients tend to decay (or explode) exponentially as they pass through $N$ layers. A residual connection adds the input directly to the next layer's output, so gradients from the output side flow through this direct path straight to the layers near the input.

$$
x_{l+1} = x_l + F(x_l)
$$

This makes it possible to train networks approaching 100 layers, such as GPT-3 (96 layers) and LLaMA 2 70B (80 layers).

#### Post-LN vs Pre-LN

<PreLNvsPostLN />

| | Post-LN (Original) | Pre-LN (Modern) |
|---|---|---|
| Norm placement | After sublayer | Before sublayer |
| Norm method | LayerNorm | LayerNorm (GPT-3) → RMSNorm (LLaMA+) |
| Training stability | Requires learning-rate warmup | Stable; converges easily even at scale |
| Adopted by | Original Transformer, BERT | GPT-3, LLaMA, Mistral |

RMSNorm (Root Mean Square Layer Normalization) is a simplified LayerNorm that skips mean subtraction:

$$
\text{RMSNorm}(x) = \frac{x}{\text{RMS}(x)} \cdot \gamma, \quad \text{RMS}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i^2}
$$

Skipping the mean makes it cheaper to compute, and in experiments it matched LayerNorm's performance, so it has become standard from LLaMA onward.

### 4. Feed-Forward Network (FFN)

The FFN is a fully connected network applied to each token's vector independently. Attention captures relationships between tokens; the FFN transforms the features of each token. FFN parameters make up roughly 2/3 of a Transformer's parameters (excluding embeddings) and are thought to store much of the knowledge the model learns.

#### Standard FFN

The original Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) used ReLU; GPT and BERT switched to GELU:

$$
\text{FFN}(x) = \text{GELU}(x W_1 + b_1) W_2 + b_2
$$

The intermediate dimension $d_{\text{ff}}$ is typically $4 \times d_{\text{model}}$ (e.g., $d = 4096$ → $d_{\text{ff}} = 16384$).

#### SwiGLU — The Modern Standard

[Shazeer (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05202) proposed SwiGLU, which combines a Gated Linear Unit with Swish activation. LLaMA and PaLM, among others, use it.

<SwiGLUDiagram />

$$
\text{SwiGLU}(x) = \bigl(\text{Swish}(x W_1) \odot (x W_3)\bigr) W_2
$$

SwiGLU improves on the standard FFN in two ways. First, $x W_3$ acts as a gate that controls, depending on the input, how much of each component of $\text{Swish}(x W_1)$ passes through. Second, $\text{Swish}(x) = x \cdot \sigma(x)$ is smooth, unlike ReLU, and returns small nonzero values for negative inputs, so gradients are less likely to vanish.

Because SwiGLU uses three matrices ($W_1, W_2, W_3$), the intermediate dimension is set to $d_{\text{ff}} = \frac{8}{3} d_{\text{model}}$ to keep the parameter count equal to a standard FFN (which uses $4 \times d_{\text{model}}$).

### 5. Output Layer

The output of the last Transformer block is normalized (with RMSNorm in LLaMA-family models) and projected to vocabulary size $|V|$ by a linear transformation (unembedding). Softmax then converts the result to a probability distribution over the next token.

Many LLMs use <strong>weight tying</strong>: $W_U = W_E^T$, sharing the embedding matrix $W_E$ with the output weight $W_U$. This removes $|V| \times d$ parameters and makes the input and output use the same semantic space.

### Causal Mask in Decoders

In decoder-only models like GPT, a <strong>causal mask</strong> blocks attention to future tokens. The token at position $i$ can attend only to positions $1, 2, \ldots, i$.

<CausalMaskDiagram />

In practice, the entries above the diagonal of the score matrix are set to $-\infty$, which become zero after softmax. Because each position sees only earlier tokens during training as well, training matches the conditions of autoregressive generation, which produces one token at a time from left to right.

## Self-Attention — The Core of Transformers

Self-attention computes, from the input, how relevant each token is to every other token, and uses those scores to build context-aware representations.

### Formula

$$
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
$$

- <strong>$Q$</strong> (Query): What each token is "looking for"
- <strong>$K$</strong> (Key): What each token "contains" (how it wants to be found)
- <strong>$V$</strong> (Value): The actual information each token passes along
- <strong>$\sqrt{d_k}$</strong>: Scaling factor to prevent dot products from growing too large, which would saturate softmax

### Advantages of Self-Attention over RNNs

1. <strong>Long-range dependencies</strong>: In an RNN, information from distant tokens fades; self-attention accesses every token directly
2. <strong>Parallel computation</strong>: An RNN processes tokens one after another; self-attention is a set of matrix operations that GPUs compute in parallel
3. <strong>Interpretability</strong>: Visualizing attention weights shows which tokens each token weighted most heavily

### Computational Complexity

Self-attention computes a score for every pair of tokens, so its cost is $O(n^2)$ in the sequence length $n$, which becomes expensive for long contexts. The techniques below improve the speed, memory use, or distribution across devices of this computation.

| Technique | Complexity | Summary |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Standard attention | $O(n^2)$ | Computes all token pairs |
| Flash Attention | $O(n^2)$ (memory $O(n)$) | Reduces reads and writes to GPU memory (HBM) |
| Multi-Query Attention | $O(n^2)$ | Shares K,V heads to speed up inference |
| Grouped-Query Attention | $O(n^2)$ | Between MQA and MHA; used in LLaMA 2 70B and others |
| Ring Attention | $O(n^2 / p)$ | Splits the sequence across $p$ devices and computes in parallel |

## Text Generation — Next Token Prediction

Given a context (an input token sequence), an LLM outputs a probability distribution over the next token. It picks one token from this distribution, appends it to the context, and repeats the process to generate text. This is <strong>autoregressive generation</strong>.

### Generation Strategies

<strong>Greedy decoding</strong>: Always pick the highest-probability token. The output is deterministic but tends to be monotonous.

<strong>Temperature scaling</strong>: Divide the logits by temperature $T$ before applying softmax.

$$
p_i = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)}
$$

- $T < 1$: Distribution sharpens → more deterministic (good for factual tasks)
- $T > 1$: Distribution flattens → more diverse/creative (good for creative writing)
- $T \to 0$: Converges to greedy decoding

<strong>Top-k sampling</strong>: Keep only the top $k$ tokens by probability, zero out the rest, and renormalize.

<strong>Top-p (Nucleus) sampling</strong>: Keep the most probable tokens, in order, until their cumulative probability exceeds $p$ (e.g., 0.95). A sharp distribution leaves few candidates and a flat one leaves many, which makes top-p more flexible than top-k with its fixed candidate count.

## Training Pipeline — From Raw Model to Conversational AI

<TrainingPipeline />

### Stage 1: Pre-training

To acquire general language knowledge, the model learns to predict the next token on a corpus of trillions of tokens, including web text, books, and code. The loss sums, over all positions, the negative log of the probability the model assigned to the correct next token $x_t$.

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pretrain}} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(x_t | x_{<t}; \theta)
$$

At this stage the model can continue a piece of text, but it has not been trained to answer questions or follow instructions.

### Stage 2: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

To teach the model to follow instructions, SFT fine-tunes it on a dataset of high-quality (instruction, response) pairs (about 13,000 in InstructGPT). From this it learns the format of answering a user's question.

```text
[Instruction] What is the capital of Japan?
[Response] The capital of Japan is Tokyo.
```

### Stage 3: RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)

A model after SFT can still produce answers not grounded in fact, or harmful output. RLHF uses human judgments of which answer is better to align the model's output with human preferences and reduce such output.

1. <strong>Train a reward model</strong>: Human annotators rank several answers to the same prompt, and that data is used to train a reward model that scores how good an answer is
2. <strong>PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)</strong>: Update the policy (LLM) to maximize the reward model's score, with a KL penalty that keeps it from drifting too far from the original model

$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{RLHF}} = \mathbb{E}\bigl[R(x, y) - \beta \, \text{KL}\bigl(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr)\bigr]
$$

$\beta$ is a coefficient that sets the strength of the KL penalty.

DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) simplifies RLHF by optimizing the policy directly from preference data without explicitly training a reward model. LLaMA 2 itself used PPO-based RLHF, but DPO has since been widely adopted in open models such as Zephyr and LLaMA 3.

## Modern LLM Techniques

### KV Cache

In autoregressive generation, the K and V of earlier tokens do not change as generation proceeds. <strong>KV caching</strong> stores the K and V computed once and reuses them at the next step. Each step then only has to compute the new token, which speeds up inference considerably, but the cache's memory use grows linearly with sequence length.

### Quantization

Quantization converts LLM parameters from 32/16-bit floating point to 4/8-bit integers to reduce memory and compute costs. Methods such as [GPTQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323) and [AWQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978) keep the accuracy loss small on many workloads. As a result, running a 70B model on a single high-memory GPU becomes practical. Whether it actually fits depends on the KV-cache size for the context length and on latency requirements.

### MoE (Mixture of Experts)

MoE splits the FFN layers into multiple "experts," and a router selects a few of them for each token (2 of 8 in [Mixtral](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04088)). The total parameter count is large, but each token uses only part of the parameters at inference time, so the model can grow without a matching growth in compute.

### Context Length Extension

Early Transformer-based models had context lengths of about 512 tokens; some models today can process over 1 million tokens. Techniques used for this include RoPE frequency scaling, YaRN, and Ring Attention.

## Summary

The main points of each section:

1. <strong>History</strong>: Word2Vec → Transformer → BERT/GPT → scaling laws driving larger models
2. <strong>Tokenization</strong>: BPE subword splitting converts arbitrary text to numerical sequences
3. <strong>Transformer</strong>: N stacked layers of self-attention + FFN + residual connections
4. <strong>Self-attention</strong>: $\text{softmax}(QK^T/\sqrt{d_k})V$ computes relevance across all tokens from the input
5. <strong>Text generation</strong>: Temperature, top-k, top-p sampling for stochastic autoregressive generation
6. <strong>Training pipeline</strong>: Pre-training → SFT → RLHF/DPO turns a "next-token predictor" into a "conversational AI"

At every stage, what the model outputs is a probability distribution over the next token. Scaled up far enough, this simple-looking mechanism makes possible the code writing and question answering mentioned at the start.
