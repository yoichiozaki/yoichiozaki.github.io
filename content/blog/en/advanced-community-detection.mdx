## Introduction — Assumptions Shared by Part 1's Methods

[Part 1](/en/blog/community-detection) covered the fundamentals of Community Detection: the history, Modularity Q formulation, standard algorithms (Louvain, Leiden, Infomap, SBM), and fundamental difficulties like the Resolution Limit and detectability threshold.

The methods in Part 1 implicitly assume three things:

1. <strong>Only pairwise edges</strong> — Communities defined solely by whether edges exist between pairs of nodes
2. <strong>Static (single snapshot) graphs</strong> — The network is assumed not to change over time
3. <strong>Single type of relationship</strong> — Only one kind of connection between nodes

Real-world networks break all three. In social networks, triadic closure ("a friend of a friend is a friend") matters, relationships change over time, and the same people may be linked by friendship, work, and shared hobbies at once.

This article covers three lines of research, each of which drops one of these assumptions:

- Higher-Order community detection — Motifs, hypergraphs, simplicial complexes
- Dynamic network community detection — Tracking communities in evolving graphs
- Multiplex network community detection — Integrating multiple relationship layers

## Part I: Higher-Order Community Detection

### Why Edges Alone Are Insufficient

Most algorithms from Part 1, including Modularity-based methods, define communities as "subgraphs with dense edges." That definition cannot tell these two graphs apart:

- <strong>Graph A</strong>: 6 nodes, 9 edges. Every edge belongs to a triangle (3 triangles total)
- <strong>Graph B</strong>: 6 nodes, 9 edges. Zero triangles. A complete bipartite graph $K_{3,3}$

<EdgeVsTriangleDiagram />

With the same node and edge counts, the two have the same edge density. Graph A, however, has strong local cohesion from triadic closure — in social-network terms, friends of friends become friends. Graph B has no triangles at all.

Methods that look only at edge density cannot see this difference. That is why researchers turned to methods that account for <strong>higher-order structures</strong> such as triangles.

### Clique Percolation Method (CPM) — An Early Higher-Order Method

One of the earliest well-known methods built on higher-order structures is the Clique Percolation Method (CPM) by Palla, Derényi, Farkas & Vicsek (2005). CPM finds communities as follows:

1. Enumerate all $k$-cliques (complete subgraphs on $k$ nodes) in the network
2. Consider two $k$-cliques "adjacent" if they share $k-1$ nodes
3. Define a community as the union of all $k$-cliques reachable through adjacent $k$-cliques

For example, in a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, applying CPM with $k=4$ uses sets of four mutually interacting proteins as building blocks to detect larger functional modules.

CPM can also detect overlapping communities, where a node belongs to several communities at once. Chains of $k$-cliques can share nodes, so a node that sits in several chains belongs to each chain's community. In social networks one person usually belongs to work colleagues, university friends, and a hobby group at the same time, so this property fits real structure.

Results depend heavily on the choice of $k$, though, and in sparse networks $k$-cliques may barely exist. CPM is also limited to $k$-cliques as its higher-order structure. The motif-based methods covered next generalize this to arbitrary network motifs.

### Network Motifs: Foundations

A <strong>network motif</strong> is a small subgraph pattern that appears statistically significantly often in a network. The concept was established by Uri Alon and colleagues (Milo et al., 2002), who showed that patterns such as feed-forward loops appear far more often in E. coli gene regulatory networks than in random networks.

<MotifCatalog />

Motifs are worth looking at because they carry functional meaning:

- <strong>Triangles</strong>: Trust and information transfer efficiency in social networks. "If A trusts B and B trusts C, then A is likely to trust C"
- <strong>Feed-forward loops</strong>: Signal filtering in gene regulation
- <strong>4-cycles</strong>: Foundation for collaborative filtering in bipartite networks (user–product)

### Benson-Gleich-Leskovec: Motif-Based Community Detection (2016)

A widely cited work on higher-order community detection is Benson, Gleich & Leskovec's "Higher-order organization of complex networks" (Science, 2016). The basic idea:

> Define communities based on <strong>motif density</strong>, not edge density.

#### The Central Measure: Motif Conductance

Conductance, as defined in Part 1:

$$
\phi(S) = \frac{\text{cut}(S, \bar{S})}{\min(\text{vol}(S), \text{vol}(\bar{S}))}
$$

Benson et al. extended this to Motif Conductance for motif $M$:

$$
\phi_M(S) = \frac{\text{cut}_M(S, \bar{S})}{\min(\text{vol}_M(S), \text{vol}_M(\bar{S}))}
$$

Where:

- $\text{cut}_M(S, \bar{S})$: Number of motif $M$ instances with nodes in both $S$ and $\bar{S}$ ("boundary-crossing motifs")
- $\text{vol}_M(S)$: Sum of motif-weighted degrees of nodes in $S$. Formally, $\text{vol}_M(S) = \sum_{i \in S} d_i^{(M)}$, where $d_i^{(M)} = \sum_j W_{ij}^{(M)}$ (row sum of the motif-weighted adjacency matrix defined below)

A smaller $\phi_M(S)$ means motif $M$ is dense inside community $S$ and few motif instances cross its boundary.

#### Motif-Weighted Adjacency Matrix

Finding the set that exactly minimizes Motif Conductance is computationally hard, so Benson et al. construct the motif-weighted adjacency matrix $W^{(M)}$ and approximate it with a spectral method:

$$
W^{(M)}_{ij} = \text{(number of instances of motif } M \text{ containing nodes } i \text{ and } j \text{ in the chosen anchor set)}
$$

<MotifWeightedAdjacencyDiagram />

For edge-anchored motifs such as triangles this often coincides with counting motif instances containing the pair $(i,j)$, but for general motifs the anchor-set definition matters. This matrix is used to build the motif-weighted Laplacian for spectral clustering:

$$
L^{(M)} = D^{(M)} - W^{(M)}
$$

where $D^{(M)}$ is the diagonal degree matrix of $W^{(M)}$.

Benson et al. proved a Cheeger-like inequality for its normalized form $\mathcal{L}^{(M)} = (D^{(M)})^{-1/2} L^{(M)} (D^{(M)})^{-1/2}$ (Benson et al., 2016). For motifs with three anchor nodes, such as triangles, Motif Conductance equals ordinary Conductance on the graph weighted by $W^{(M)}$, and the following holds:

$$
\frac{\lambda_2^{(M)}}{2} \leq \phi_M^* \leq \sqrt{2 \lambda_2^{(M)}}
$$

where $\lambda_2^{(M)}$ is the second smallest eigenvalue of $\mathcal{L}^{(M)}$ and $\phi_M^*$ is the optimal Motif Conductance. The cluster $S$ found by the spectral method satisfies $\phi_M(S) \leq 4\sqrt{\phi_M^*}$, so its distance from the optimum is bounded. This mirrors the classical Cheeger inequality for spectral clustering from Part 1.

#### Advantages of Motif-Based Methods

Compared to edge-based methods, motif-based methods offer:

1. <strong>More information</strong>: In addition to pairwise edges, they use patterns of 3 or more nodes
2. <strong>Domain knowledge integration</strong>: Choose motifs suited to your domain — feed-forward loops for biology, triangles for social networks
3. <strong>Noise robustness</strong>: Random edges rarely form motifs, so motif density is less affected by random noise

### Hypergraph Community Detection

Motif-based methods count higher-order patterns built from a graph's edges. Hypergraph-based approaches instead represent multi-body relationships directly in the data.

In standard graphs, edges connect exactly 2 nodes. In hypergraphs, a <strong>hyperedge</strong> directly connects any number of nodes:

$$
e = \{v_1, v_2, ..., v_k\}, \quad k \geq 2
$$

Examples of multi-body relationships that hyperedges can represent:

- Paper co-authorship (3+ authors)
- Email CC (multiple recipients)
- Chemical reactions (multiple reactants)

For example, in the DBLP co-authorship network, each paper with 3+ authors can be represented as one hyperedge.

The main approaches to community detection on hypergraphs are:

<strong>Clique Expansion</strong>: Convert each hyperedge $e = \{v_1, ..., v_k\}$ into $\binom{k}{2}$ pairwise edges and process as a standard graph. This conversion loses the fact that everyone was involved at the same time: 4 people in a single meeting (one hyperedge) and 4 people having 6 separate pairwise conversations (6 edges) look identical after conversion.

<strong>Direct Hypergraph Methods</strong>: Spectral clustering on hypergraphs uses the hypergraph Laplacian introduced by Zhou, Huang & Schölkopf (2006):

$$
\Delta_H = D_v - H W_e D_e^{-1} H^T
$$

where $H$ is the incidence matrix (node × hyperedge), $D_v$ is the node degree matrix, $W_e$ is the hyperedge weight matrix, and $D_e$ is the diagonal matrix of hyperedge degrees (sizes).

Kumar et al. (2020) proposed a method that keeps the hyperedges intact while iteratively maximizing a weighted modularity whose definition reflects hyperedge sizes.

### Simplicial Complex Approaches

A simplicial complex is a concept from topology that can be viewed as a hypergraph with an added inclusion condition. A $k$-simplex is a set of $k+1$ nodes, and a simplicial complex also contains every subset of each of its simplices as a simplex.

- 0-simplex: node
- 1-simplex: edge
- 2-simplex: triangle (3 nodes + 3 edges)
- $k$-simplex: complete graph on $(k+1)$ nodes (plus all faces)

On a simplicial complex, boundary operators define the Hodge Laplacian, which measures the "smoothness" of $k$-chains. Since 1-chains correspond to flows on edges, it can be used for community detection based on edge flows. Schaub et al. (2020) connected random walks on simplicial complexes to the normalized Hodge 1-Laplacian.

The methods in Part I widened the kinds of relationships that can be represented: edges → cliques → motifs → hyperedges → simplices. All of them, however, work on static graphs that do not change over time.

## Part II: Dynamic Network Community Detection

### Formulating Dynamic Networks

A network whose structure changes over time — friendships forming and dissolving, papers being published and cited, protein expression patterns shifting — is called a dynamic network.

Two main representations exist:

<strong>Discrete snapshot model</strong>: A sequence of graph snapshots $G_1, G_2, ..., G_T$ at times $t_1, t_2, ..., t_T$. Each $G_t = (V_t, E_t)$ is a static graph.

<strong>Continuous-time model</strong>: Edge appearances and disappearances treated as timestamped events $(i, j, t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}})$. Retains more detail but complicates analysis.

The snapshot model is widely used in practice, but results can change considerably with the time granularity (snapshot interval).

### Community Lifecycle

In dynamic networks it is not enough to find communities at each time step; communities must also be <strong>tracked through time</strong>. Communities go through lifecycle events such as these:

These events are identified by matching communities across consecutive snapshots.

### Approach 1: Independent Detection + Matching (Two-Stage)

The simplest method applies community detection independently to each snapshot, then matches communities across consecutive time steps:

```text
Two-Stage Approach:
  1. Detect communities C_t = {C_t^1, C_t^2, ...} independently at each time t
  2. Match between adjacent times t, t+1 using Jaccard coefficient:
     J(C_t^i, C_{t+1}^j) = |C_t^i ∩ C_{t+1}^j| / |C_t^i ∪ C_{t+1}^j|
  3. Identify lifecycle events from matching:
     - C_t^i with no match → Death
     - C_{t+1}^j with no source → Birth
     - 1-to-many → Split
     - Many-to-1 → Merge
```

This approach lets you use static community detection algorithms unchanged. Louvain, Leiden, Infomap, or any other method from Part 1 can be applied to each snapshot.

On the other hand, <strong>temporal coherence</strong> is not guaranteed. A few edges added or removed can change the detected community structure substantially, so communities that are actually stable may appear to fluctuate wildly.

### Approach 2: Evolutionary Clustering (2006)

Chakrabarti, Kumar & Tomkins (KDD 2006) proposed Evolutionary Clustering, which builds temporal coherence into the objective function.

Specifically, it maximizes a weighted sum of fit to the current snapshot and similarity to the previous partition:

$$
\mathcal{S}_t = \alpha \cdot \text{quality}(C_t, G_t) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{similarity}(C_t, C_{t-1})
$$

- $\text{quality}(C_t, G_t)$: Quality of partition $C_t$ for graph $G_t$ (e.g., Modularity Q)
- $\text{similarity}(C_t, C_{t-1})$: Similarity to previous partition (e.g., NMI, Jaccard)
- $\alpha \in [0, 1]$: Trade-off between "fitting current data" and "consistency with past"

$\alpha = 1$ means fully independent detection (ignoring history); $\alpha = 0$ means keeping the exact same structure (ignoring new data).

The practical difficulty is setting $\alpha$. During periods of rapid change (e.g., a global event on social media) $\alpha$ should be large, and during stable periods small, but deciding when to switch is itself hard.

Evolutionary Clustering was the first systematic formulation of dynamic community detection as an explicit optimization problem, influencing many subsequent methods.

### Approach 3: Multislice Modularity (Mucha et al., 2010)

Mucha, Richardson, Macon, Porter & Onnela's "Community Structure in Time-Dependent, Multiscale, and Multiplex Networks" (Science, 2010) introduced one of the most widely used frameworks for dynamic community detection.

The method stacks time snapshots as "slices" and treats them as one large graph in which copies of the same node are linked by inter-slice coupling edges. On this graph it defines Multislice Modularity:

$$
Q_{\text{multislice}} = \frac{1}{2\mu} \sum_{ijsr} \left[ \left( A_{ijs} - \gamma_s \frac{k_{is} k_{js}}{2m_s} \right) \delta_{sr} + \delta_{ij} \omega_{jsr} \right] \delta(g_{is}, g_{jr})
$$

What each symbol and term means:

<strong>Index meanings:</strong>
- $i, j$: nodes
- $s, r$: slices (time step or layer index)
- $g_{is}$: community assignment of node $i$ in slice $s$

<strong>Term 1:</strong> $\left( A_{ijs} - \gamma_s \frac{k_{is} k_{js}}{2m_s} \right) \delta_{sr}$

This is the "intra-slice" Modularity. The $\delta_{sr}$ restricts it to same-slice pairs ($s = r$). $\gamma_s$ is the resolution parameter (from Part 1), which can vary per slice.

<strong>Term 2:</strong> $\delta_{ij} \omega_{jsr}$

This is the term Mucha et al.'s formulation adds. $\delta_{ij}$ restricts it to the "same node," and $\omega_{jsr}$ is the inter-slice coupling strength. Intuitively, it "connects node $i$ at time $s$ to the same node $i$ at time $r$ with a virtual edge."

<strong>Choices for $\omega_{jsr}$:</strong>
- Couple only temporally adjacent snapshots: $\omega_{jsr} = \omega$ if $|s - r| = 1$
- Couple all snapshots uniformly: $\omega_{jsr} = \omega$ for all $s \neq r$
- Node- and time-dependent coupling: $\omega_{jsr}$ depends on $j, s, r$

Larger $\omega$ strengthens temporal coherence ("the same node should stay in the same community across time"); smaller $\omega$ gives more independence to each snapshot.

<strong>Normalization:</strong> $2\mu = \sum_{jr} \kappa_{jr}$, where $\kappa_{jr} = k_{jr} + c_{jr}$ (node $j$'s degree in slice $r$ plus its inter-slice coupling strength)

This formulation has the following properties:

1. <strong>Unifies dynamic and multiplex networks</strong>: Interpret slices as "time steps" for dynamic, or "layers" for multiplex
2. <strong>Reuses existing methods</strong>: This extended Modularity can be optimized with Louvain/Leiden-style greedy algorithms
3. <strong>Multiscale analysis</strong>: Varying $\gamma_s$ and $\omega$ explores structures at different scales

### Approach 4: Tensor Decomposition

De Domenico et al. (2013) proposed a mathematical framework representing multiplex/dynamic networks as tensors.

A multiplex network with $L$ layers (or time snapshots) can be represented as a 4th-order tensor $\mathcal{M}$:

$$
\mathcal{M}_{ij\alpha\beta}
$$

- $i, j$: nodes
- $\alpha, \beta$: layers
- $\mathcal{M}_{ij\alpha\alpha}$: edge weight between $i$ and $j$ in layer $\alpha$
- $\mathcal{M}_{ii\alpha\beta}$: coupling of node $i$ between layers $\alpha$ and $\beta$

Tensor decomposition (CP or Tucker decomposition) is applied to this tensor to extract community structure. It is the multi-dimensional counterpart of matrix decomposition (PCA, NMF) and captures structure in both node and layer dimensions at once.

The tensor representation is mathematically general but scales poorly to large networks. Gauvin, Panisson & Cattuto (2014) applied non-negative tensor factorization (NTF) to face-to-face contact networks and estimated community membership and temporal activity patterns together.

If slices are read as relationship types rather than time steps, Multislice Modularity and tensor decomposition apply unchanged to multiplex networks.

## Part III: Multiplex Network Community Detection

### What Is a Multiplex Network?

A <strong>multiplex network</strong> has multiple types of relationships (layers) among the same set of nodes.

<MultiplexNetworkDiagram />

Examples:
- Social media: "follow," "message," "like" as separate layers
- Transportation: "rail," "bus," "air" as separate layers
- Biology: "protein-protein interaction," "gene regulation," "metabolic pathway" as separate layers

Multiplex networks are a kind of multilayer network. The general formulation of multilayer networks is summarized in Kivelä et al.'s (2014) survey; those whose node set is the same across all layers are called multiplex networks.

### Approach 1: Layer Aggregation

The simplest method aggregates all layer adjacency matrices into a single graph and applies standard community detection:

$$
A_{\text{agg}} = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{L} w_\alpha A^{(\alpha)}
$$

where $A^{(\alpha)}$ is layer $\alpha$'s adjacency matrix and $w_\alpha$ are weights.

This is easy to implement but <strong>loses layer-specific information</strong>. If a node belongs to community A in one layer and community B in another, the aggregated graph keeps no trace of that difference.

### Approach 2: Multislice Modularity (Revisited)

The Multislice Modularity from Part II (Mucha et al., 2010) applies to multiplex networks simply by reading slices as "layers."

The coupling parameter $\omega$ controls cross-layer community consistency. Larger $\omega$ means the same node tends to belong to the same community across all layers.

### Approach 3: Layer-Coupled Optimization

Interdonato, Tagarelli, Ienco, Sallaberry & Roche (2017) categorize multiplex community detection into three approaches:

1. <strong>Layer-independent</strong>: Detect independently per layer, merge results
2. <strong>Aggregation-based</strong>: Pre-aggregate layers
3. <strong>Layer-coupled</strong>: Optimize across all layers simultaneously

The third, layer-coupled category uses information from all layers at once, at a higher computational cost.

### GenLouvain: Practical Multislice Implementation

GenLouvain (Jutla, Jeub & Mucha, 2011–) is a widely used implementation for optimizing Multislice Modularity:

```text
GenLouvain:
  Input: Multiplex/dynamic network (adjacency matrices A^(s), coupling ω)
  1. Build Multislice Modularity matrix B:
     B_{isjs} = A_{ijs} - γ_s * k_{is}*k_{js}/(2*m_s)  (intra-slice)
     B_{isir} = ω_{isr}                                  (inter-slice, same node)
  2. Apply Louvain local-moving + aggregation on matrix B
  3. Maximize Q_multislice
  → Community assignment for each node in each slice
```

## Part IV: The Current Frontier

### GNN-Based Community Detection

Research on Graph Neural Network (GNN)-based community detection has grown in the 2020s.

<strong>DMoN (Tsitsulin et al., 2023)</strong>: Relaxes Modularity into a differentiable form and predicts the community assignment matrix directly from node embeddings produced by a GNN encoder. The loss function:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2m} \text{Tr}(C^T B C) + \frac{\sqrt{k}}{n} \left\| C^T \mathbf{1}_n \right\| - 1
$$

- Term 1: Modularity maximization (negated for minimization)
- Term 2: Collapse regularization. $C^T \mathbf{1}_n$ is the cluster size vector $\mathbf{s}$ (number of nodes assigned to each cluster). Minimized (value 0) when all clusters have equal size ($s_i = n/k$); penalizes concentration of all nodes into one cluster
- $C$: Soft community assignment matrix ($n \times k$) output by the GNN
- $B$: Modularity matrix ($B_{ij} = A_{ij} - k_i k_j / 2m$)

<strong>MinCutPool (Bianchi et al., 2020)</strong>: A graph pooling layer trained with a relaxed normalized-cut loss. Stacking pooling layers groups nodes hierarchically.

GNN-based methods can use node features — social media profiles, paper text, protein sequences — together with graph structure. Challenges remain:

- <strong>Interpretability</strong>: GNN intermediate representations are opaque
- <strong>Generalization</strong>: Unclear whether models transfer to structurally different graphs
- <strong>Scalability</strong>: Training costs on large graphs

### Flow-Based Dynamic Community Detection

Some work handles changing communities by following the flow of information. Rosvall & Bergstrom (2010) clustered each snapshot with Infomap, assessed the significance of the communities by bootstrap resampling, and visualized changes between time points with alluvial diagrams.

Inter-slice coupling was built into the Map Equation by the multilayer Map Equation of De Domenico, Lancichinetti, Arenas & Rosvall (2015). A random walker moves within a slice and, with some probability, moves to the same node in another slice. It expresses the same kind of inter-slice coupling as Mucha et al.'s Multislice Modularity, but in an information-theoretic framework.

### Streaming Algorithms

Community detection in streaming settings, where edges arrive one at a time, is another line of research, aimed mainly at real-time detection in large social networks.

OSLOM (Lancichinetti, Radicchi, Ramasco & Fortunato, 2011) is primarily a method for finding statistically significant communities in static graphs. Its local order-statistics test can be reused in incremental or dynamic settings, but repeated p-value testing makes it comparatively expensive, so it is not a lightweight streaming algorithm.

## Conclusion — How Community Detection Has Developed

The research covered in Part 1 and this article falls roughly into three generations.

<strong>Generation 1</strong> (1970s–early 2000s): From graph partitioning to social network analysis. The Girvan-Newman method and the Modularity formulation. The "static, single-layer, pairwise" framework based on edge density.

<strong>Generation 2</strong> (late 2000s–early 2010s): Louvain's scalability (with its refinement, Leiden, following in 2019), SBM statistical inference, and the discovery of the Resolution Limit and detectability threshold. Methods improved, and the difficulty of the problem itself became clearer.

<strong>Generation 3</strong> (from the mid-2000s, especially late 2010s–present): Methods that use information beyond edge density:
- Higher-order structure (motifs, hypergraphs, simplicial complexes)
- Temporal dynamics (Evolutionary Clustering, Multislice Modularity)
- Multiple relationship types (multiplex networks, tensor approaches)
- Deep learning (GNN-based methods)

Community Detection has no single correct answer. Edge density, motif density, information compression efficiency, statistical significance, temporal coherence, and cross-layer consistency each correspond to a different definition of "community." Each method is a tool for looking at network structure from a different angle.

Active research directions include:

1. <strong>Hypergraph GNNs</strong>: Unsupervised community detection using Hypergraph Neural Networks (HGNN)
2. <strong>Causal communities</strong>: Defining communities based on node/edge temporal causal relationships
3. <strong>Scalable dynamic methods</strong>: Real-time processing at billion-node scale
4. <strong>Extended theoretical guarantees</strong>: Detectability thresholds for higher-order and dynamic settings

Part 1's conclusion — "perfect Community Detection is fundamentally impossible" — applies even more strongly in higher-order, dynamic, and multiplex settings, because the choice of motif and of $\alpha$ or $\omega$ changes the results. In exchange, these methods can analyze structure that edge density alone cannot capture: triadic closure, change over time, and multiple types of relationships. The next article continues from the dynamic-network discussion here to the stricter [streaming setting, where edges arrive or disappear one event at a time](/en/blog/streaming-community-detection).
